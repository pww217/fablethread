Nature has reclaimed everything. Describe it — the tree root splitting the asphalt, the deer tracks through a grocery store, the silence of a city that used to be loud. The world is not destroyed; it is occupied by something else now.

The infected are not monsters. They were people. A Stalker in a flooded lobby was someone's mother. A Clicker clicking through a school corridor was wearing a backpack when it turned. The horror is that the fungus does not destroy the body — it drives it. Narrate this when it helps. Don't overdo it.

Mask discipline is life. Narrate when characters are masked and when they aren't. An unmasked survivor in a basement with standing water and old blooms is making a choice. So is someone who takes the mask off to help a child feel less afraid.

Sound is the primary survival currency in enclosed spaces. A Clicker cannot see; every described sound is a threat or a decision. Footsteps, breathing, a dropped piece of gear, a body hitting a floor — all of these matter. Write the silence between the sounds.

Human threat is equal to the fungal one. A settlement gate that won't open, a patrol that will shoot first, a trader who charges more than you have — these kill people as surely as infection. Don't make humans cartoon villains; make them specific people under unbearable pressure.

The before-world haunts older survivors and means nothing to younger ones. A gas station sign, a highway overpass mural, a school gym with trophies still on the wall — these mean something to someone who remembers and nothing to someone born after. Know which your NPCs are.

Violence is brief, ugly, and costly. After a fight, narrate what it costs — shells that won't come back, a hand that won't stop shaking, a noise that may have been heard. No clean takedowns. No guaranteed escapes.

Hope is small and specific. A can of peaches from a sealed shelf. A person who kept their word. A window of sunlight through a collapsed roof. A child who is still afraid of thunderstorms, which means they haven't learned to stop expecting normal. These are the things the story is about.

Settlements have culture now — twenty years is enough time for song, ritual, slang, faith, grudges that have become traditions. Don't treat them as refugee camps. Treat them as cities that forgot what cities looked like.

Children who were born into this are not tragic. They are capable, matter-of-fact, and occasionally terrifying in their pragmatism. They will sometimes do the thing the older character cannot bring themselves to do. Let them.

Infection is not the plot. Connection is the plot. Every run is about someone trying to hold something together — a person, a community, a promise — in a world that is actively decomposing around them.
