# World Bible — Allied 1940–1945

## The War

The war is a machine of logistics. Ammunition, fuel, spare parts, food, medical supplies — the side that moves them best wins. The Allied war effort is held together by supply officers, quartermasters, transport drivers, and the civilians who keep the factories running.

The year shifts depending on theater: 1940 in North Africa and the Mediterranean, 1942 in the Pacific, 1943–1944 in Europe and the Pacific, 1945 as the end draws near. The world adapts to whichever year the scenario picks.

## Allied Structure

The Allies are united by necessity, not ideology. American, British, Canadian, Australian, Free French, Dutch, Polish, Australian, New Zealand, and other forces operate under different chains of command, with different doctrines, different equipment, and different relationships to each other. Cooperation is real but strained.

US forces: standardized equipment, massive logistics, doctrine built on firepower and paper. British forces: rationed everything, improvisation valued, intelligence services well-resourced. Canadian, Australian, New Zealand forces: experienced, often undersupplied, fiercely independent. Free European forces: fighting for homelands that still exist on paper.

## The Enemy

The Axis powers — Germany, Japan, Italy — are organized, well-supplied, and ruthless. They are not monstrous caricatures. They are soldiers and civilians following orders, believing in their cause, making the same calculations about survival and duty that the Allies make.

The enemy's strength is organization and doctrine. Their weakness is overextension and the same logistics problem the Allies face.

## Theater Flexibility

This pack is theater-agnostic. The world bible covers common ground that applies regardless of location:

- Communication: field telephones, radios (often broken or intercepted), messengers, flags, flares. Orders travel slowly. Miscommunication is common.
- Mobility: trucks, jeeps, landing craft, transport aircraft, bicycles, horses. Fuel is always constrained. Roads are always congested.
- Combat: artillery dominates. Small arms fire is short and desperate. Air support is infrequent and hard to call.
- Morale: soldiers fight for the person next to them, not for flags or ideologies. Fear is normal. Cowardice exists. Courage is ordinary.

## The Human Cost

Casualties are measured in replaced individuals, not numbers. A killed soldier is a name, a letter home, a next-of-kin notification. The living carry the weight — not as trauma, but as routine. The job keeps going.

Civilian populations are everywhere: occupied territories, refugee columns, bombed cities, liberated villages. They have their own agendas, their own information, their own reasons for helping or hindering.

## What Still Works

Field kitchens. Field hospitals. Postal service (slow but steady). Military intelligence (fragmented, competitive). Engineering units (build bridges, roads, airfields under fire). Chaplains (underutilized but needed). The legal framework of war (Geneva Conventions, rules of engagement — followed inconsistently by everyone).

## The End

By 1945, the end is visible. For the Allied soldier, this changes everything. The question is no longer "will we win?" but "what do we do now?" The last offensives are the most dangerous — everyone is tired, eager, and careless.
