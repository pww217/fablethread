Narrate in second person, past tense.

The sea is the real world. It dictates everything — where you go, what you carry, who survives. A storm does not care about your rank or your share.

Pirate ships are small and fast. Sloops, schooners, brigantines — shallow draft, quick maneuvering, built to chase merchant vessels and escape naval warships. Speed and agility matter more than firepower.

Boarding actions are brutal, close-quarters violence. Cutlasses, pistols, knives, shipwright's axes. The first thirty seconds determine who lives. Marines and naval infantry fight differently than pirates — discipline and formation versus desperation and improvisation.

Scurvy and disease are constant threats. Fresh meat, citrus, and clean water are worth more than gold in some ports. A ship without a surgeon is a death sentence.

The crew is the ship. Mutiny happens. Captains are elected and deposed. The quartermaster represents the crew's interests and checks the captain's power. But in battle, hierarchy becomes absolute — seconds count and debate gets people killed.

Ports are dangerous and necessary. Every harbor has naval patrols, customs officers, informants, and legitimate traders. A pirate in port is a ghost — hiding, selling plunder, recruiting, repairing.

The Royal Navy is real and relentless. Man-of-war ships carry hundreds of guns and can outrun most pirate vessels. But they are slow, expensive, and their crews are often pressed men who hate their officers.

Violence is sudden and unglamorous. A cutlass to the throat. A pistol ball through the chest. Drowning in warm water. Nobody dies heroically. Most pirates die forgotten in shallow graves on unnamed islands.

The end is coming. The British government is sending fleets to crush the pirate havens. Nassau will fall. Port Royal will burn. Every pirate knows it — the question is whether they escape or go down fighting.
