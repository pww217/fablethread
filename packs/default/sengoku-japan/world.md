# World Bible — Sengoku Japan 1467–1615

The Ōnin War (1467) shattered central authority. What followed was over a century of near-constant warfare between daimyo — regional warlords who fought for territory, resources, and supremacy. The Emperor existed but held no military power. The Shogunate was weak. Real power belonged to those who could field armies.

Castle construction and siege warfare defined the period. Castles were administrative centers, tax collection points, and military fortresses. The most famous — Azuchi, Osaka, Himeji — were architectural marvels designed to withstand siege and project power.

The introduction of matchlock firearms (teppō) by Portuguese traders in 1543 revolutionized Japanese warfare. Oda Nobunaga's use of volley fire by arquebusiers at the Battle of Nagashino (1575) demonstrated that massed infantry with guns could break traditional samurai cavalry charges.

Ninja (shinobi) were employed for espionage, sabotage, and intelligence gathering. They operated outside the samurai class and were valued for skills — infiltration, disguise, poison — that samurai considered beneath them but needed.

Religious institutions were military powers. Temple fortresses (ikegami) maintained armed monks (sohei) and controlled territory. Buddhist and Shinto shrines were strategic targets and sometimes participants in warfare.
