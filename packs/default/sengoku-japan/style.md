Narrate in second person, past tense.

War in the Sengoku period is not romantic. It is mud, blood, hunger, and fear. Samurai who fight on horseback with bows and swords are a minority. Most soldiers are ashigaru infantry with spears and, increasingly, matchlock guns.

Castle siege is the dominant form of warfare. Direct assault is costly and rarely successful. Starvation, tunneling, betrayal, and cutting supply lines are the real siege weapons. A castle that surrenders intact is better than one taken by storm.

Duels between individual warriors do happen, but they are exceptions — usually in the context of a larger battle or as a challenge issued before combat. Most fighting is formation-based: spear walls, arrow volleys, coordinated charges.

Firearms are changing everything. Matchlock arquebuses are unreliable in rain, slow to reload, and inaccurate at range. But a volley of fifty guns can break a samurai charge. Daimyo who invest in gunners win.

Loyalty is pragmatic. Daimyo switch sides. Vassals defect. A warrior who serves the winning side keeps his status. A loyalist who serves the loser loses everything. The later idealization of bushido as unwavering honor is not the reality of the Sengoku period.

Night raids and surprise attacks are common. Fire is the great equalizer — a few torches can destroy a wooden castle or a village. Darkness favors the attacker who knows the terrain.

Violence is sudden and brutal. A sword strike that severs an arm. An arrow through the throat. Drowning in a moat. Burning alive. Samurai who die by seppuku are the exception, not the rule. Most warriors die anonymously on battlefields that will be forgotten.

The landscape matters. Mountains, rivers, forests, and coastal plains shape every campaign. A well-chosen position can defeat a larger force. A forced march through mountain passes can break an army.

The period moves toward unification. Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, Tokugawa Ieyasu — names that will define Japan's future. But in the moment, every daimyo thinks they are the one who will win.
