# World Bible — The Divided States (1861)

## The Calendar

April 1861. The war has begun. Fort Sumter has fallen. The Union holds the North; the Confederacy controls the South. The border states — Maryland, Delaware, Kentucky, Missouri — are split by loyalty and blood. Virginia has seceded. The Mississippi is contested. The rail lines run both ways but only one set of trains has orders.

Lincoln has called for 75,000 volunteers. The Confederacy has raised an army of farmers, merchants, and gentlemen. The Union has the Navy, the railroads, the factories. The Confederacy has the rivers, the terrain, the will to defend home ground.

## The Geography

**The North** has the population (twice the South), the factories (nine out of ten), the rail network, the Navy. But it has a finite pool of volunteers; the draft has not yet come. The capital is Washington — surrounded by hostile territory, dependent on the Potomac for supplies.

**The South** has the rivers (the Mississippi, the James, the Tennessee), the cavalry, the defensive terrain. But it has fewer factories, a blockaded coastline, and a population that includes four million enslaved people whose loyalty and location is both a resource and a liability.

**The Border** is the killing field. Kentucky is divided down the middle. Missouri has two rival governments. Western Virginia became West Virginia and does not want to be reminded of the South. The Shenandoah Valley feeds both armies.

**The West** is vast and sparsely held. The Mississippi is the strategic prize — control the river, split the Confederacy. Fort Donelson, Fort Henry, Vicksburg — the names mean nothing yet but they will matter.

## The Armies

**The Union Army** (US Army) is small but professional. Most officers resigned to join the South. The new Union volunteers are untrained, undersupplied, and eager. The Navy blockades the Southern ports. Ironclads are being built in the North.

**The Confederate Army** (CSA) is assembled from state militias and volunteer regiments. Leadership is strong — gentlemen who could have been politicians are now generals. Supply is the weakness; there is no consistent currency, no reliable supply chain, no navy.

**The Navies**: The Union Navy enforces the Anaconda Plan — blockade the Southern ports, control the Mississippi, take Atlanta. The Confederate Navy is a handful of raiders and converted cotton-clad riverboats.

## The Technology

**Rifled muskets** are standard. Smoothbore muskets are still common but being replaced. Rifled barrels double effective range. Minie balls cause devastating wounds.

**Artillery** — smoothbore howitzers and rifled Parrott guns. Ammunition is the constraint; every shot is counted and logged.

**Rail and telegraph** are the strategic assets. The North has both in abundance. The South has a few rail junctions and a telegraph network that the Union is actively tapping and destroying.

**Ironclads** are the new reality. Monitor vs. Virginia changed naval warfare. Rivers are no longer safe.

**Medicine** is pre-germ-theory. Amputation is common for limb wounds. Infection kills more than bullets. Quinine treats malaria. Morphine is the painkiller. Alcohol is the antiseptic.

## The Economy

The North prints greenbacks. The South prints bonds that are already inflating. The blockade starves the South of gunpowder, rifles, and medicine. The North has food but needs time to mobilize.

Enslaved people are property in the Confederacy and fuel in the Union — runaways are contraband, then soldiers. The labor of four million people shapes every strategy.

Rail freight moves armies. River steamers move supplies. Wagons move slowly and break often. Horse teams die of exhaustion.

## What Still Works

The rail network — fragmented but functional. The telegraph — the most powerful weapon in the war. The Mississippi steamboat trade. The river towns that trade with both sides. The field hospitals that keep operating between battles. The churches that serve both armies.

## The Nature of the War

This is not a war of clean lines and clear objectives. It is a war of rail junctions and river crossings, of supply depots and telegraph lines, of border counties where neighbor fights neighbor. The battle lines shift with the seasons. The real war is logistical — who can feed their army, replace their ammunition, sustain their volunteers.

Every town has a story. Every soldier has a reason. The war is fought with rifles and railroads and the will to keep going when the rifle runs dry and the train doesn't come.
