# World Bible — Noir 1930s

## The City

The city is a collection of overlapping jurisdictions: city police, county sheriff, state troopers, federal agents, private detectives, harbor commission, transit authority. Each has a different scope, different rules, different people on the payroll.

The city is built on layers: the downtown financial district, the immigrant neighborhoods, the manufacturing corridor, the waterfront, the suburbs beginning to spread along streetcar lines. Each layer has different laws, different economies, different relationships to authority.

## The Economy

The Great Depression is the background condition. It is not a backdrop; it is the ground. Unemployment is 20–25%. The New Deal programs are new and uncertain. The Civilian Conservation Corps, the WPA, the Federal Emergency Relief Administration — they help but they are not enough.

The economy runs on cash and favors. The black market is not a subculture; it is a significant portion of the economy. Prohibition ended in 1933, but the bootlegging infrastructure remains. Organized crime has diversified into labor racketeering, gambling, loan-sharking, protection.

## Law and Justice

The police force is a mix of idealists and careerists, with a significant portion taking some form of payback from people they are supposed to regulate. The chief rotates. The politicians rotate faster.

Private detectives are legal in some jurisdictions, illegal in others. They lack arrest power but have access to information that police won't share. They are hired by everyone: legitimate businesses, suspicious characters, people who fall between the two categories.

The courts are backlogged. Public defenders are overworked. Bail is set arbitrarily. A plea bargain is the most likely outcome for any arrest.

## The Media

Newspapers are the dominant force. Radio is growing but print still rules. A front-page story can make or break a political career, a business, a reputation. Editors are powerful and often compromised.

Radio news is trusted by some, dismissed by others. The police use it for publicity. Criminals use it for misinformation.

## The Infrastructure

The city runs on physical infrastructure: telephones (not everyone has one), telegraphs, pneumatic tubes in old buildings, delivery boys, train lines, streetcar lines, ferry routes. Physical presence still matters. A phone call can be intercepted; a face-to-face conversation cannot.

## What Still Works

The public library. The public hospital (underfunded but real). The parish system (Catholic, Jewish, Protestant — each with different roles in the community). The labor unions (growing but under pressure). The mutual aid societies (Black churches, fraternal orders, ethnic associations).

## The People

The city's population is shaped by migration: Southern blacks (Great Migration), Southern Europeans, Eastern Europeans, Mexican-Americans, Chinese-American communities. Each has different relationships to the police, to the economy, to each other.

The powerful: industrialists, politicians, union bosses, newspaper publishers, federal agents. They are not cartoon villains. They have real power and real justifications.

The powerless: factory workers, dock workers, sharecroppers, domestic workers, unemployed veterans. They are not noble victims. They make choices.
